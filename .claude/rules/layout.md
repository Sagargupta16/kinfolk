# Layout and connectors

Moved verbatim from `CLAUDE.md` on 2026-10-31. Loads when a file matching `paths:` is read or edited.

## Key files

- [lib/tree/layout.ts](lib/tree/layout.ts) -- ELK layered layout followed by household alignment: partner-connected people share one packed row, parent households rank above child households, and fixed node dimensions remain authoritative
- [lib/tree/siblings.ts](lib/tree/siblings.ts) -- every family's horizontal run in a generation gap (the sibling bracket with its stems, or a lone child's turn) and its lane, computed AFTER ELK from positions; the layout sizes each gap from them
- [lib/tree/paths.ts](lib/tree/paths.ts) -- connector geometry as strings: the marriage line, the rounded elbow, the sibling bracket, the orbit bow. Pure, so a corner that inverts can be measured rather than squinted at.
- [lib/tree/radial.ts](lib/tree/radial.ts) -- the ORBIT arrangement: one person centred, everybody else on rings by relationship distance. Answers "who surrounds this person", which the layered pedigree cannot, since there distance encodes generation.

## Gotchas

- **ELK runs in a Web Worker, and it cannot be the bundled build.** `elk.bundled.js` detects a worker scope and installs its own `self.onmessage` dispatcher instead of exporting the in-thread worker it needs, so `new ELK()` inside a worker has nothing to run on. [lib/tree/elk-worker.ts](lib/tree/elk-worker.ts) imports elkjs's real `elk-worker.min.js`, and `layout.ts` talks to it through `elk-api` with a `workerFactory` -- `new Worker(new URL("./elk-worker.ts", import.meta.url), { type: "module" })`, the one form both Vite and Turbopack resolve. `elk-api` waits on a worker that never starts forever, so the worker's first `error` rejects the layouts in flight and every later layout runs on the page thread (verified with the worker script answering 404: all 256 nodes in 1.5s). Node has no `Worker`, so tests and the bench run in-thread.
- **Pedigree rank is a household invariant applied after ELK.** `alignPedigreeRows()` in [lib/tree/layout.ts](lib/tree/layout.ts) union-finds partner-connected people, ranks the household DAG parent-before-child, preserves ELK's crossing-minimised x order, packs partners contiguously with a tighter internal gap and uses larger gaps between households. The internal partner gap never becomes narrower than the union mark plus clearance. Inside a household the members are walked ALONG THEIR MARRIAGES (`chainOrder`, 0.8.1), not left in ELK's order: ELK read Victoria Melita's household as Ernest Louis, Kirill, Eleonore, Victoria Melita -- she married the first two, he remarried the third -- so no couple stood side by side and all three marriage lines ran through the gap on one line; the walk gives Kirill, Victoria Melita, Ernest Louis, Eleonore. Somebody with three partners can stand beside only two, so true endpoint adjacency can still be impossible; `placeUnionJunctions()` then routes that marriage a third of the gap below the couple instead of drawing a rail through the intervening card, and a direct childless partnership takes the same detour (`detourY` in siblings.ts, which the canvas draws and the lanes keep clear of). Contradictory cycles take the total-render fallback rather than dropping a person. Do not replace this with directed partner layers or hardcoded generations, and keep structured nodes non-draggable or one pointer move can invalidate every rank and rail.
- **A row packed on its OWN extent throws away ELK's parent-over-children alignment, and the row must be packed deepest-first to keep it.** `alignPedigreeRows()` originally centred each generation on its own ELK extent at minimum gaps, consulting nothing below it -- so a couple sat a mean 775px from its children's midpoint (median 600) on the earlier 117-person sample tree, and on a real graph the viewer's parents were 807px right of him and his sisters. The parent row also packed to 3761px while the children it had to reach spanned 5541px, 1.47x wider, so a couple in the middle had 56px of slack against a 1445px journey and no post-hoc nudge could rescue it. Rows are therefore packed DEEPEST-FIRST (the row below is final, so a target cannot move underneath), each household is placed over its own children, and the gap tiers are a FLOOR rather than a target -- a generation is as wide as its descendants make it. Two traps inside this: a one-directional sweep CANNOT centre a family whose children lie to its left (one couple at x=8098 had children at x=5929, and pushing right pinned 16 of 34), so the row is re-ordered to follow its children before any gap is enforced and resolved by bidirectional relaxation; and perfect centring is sometimes arithmetically impossible, since the widest row needs 11228px while its children span 7854px. When it cannot hold, the minimum gap wins -- an off-centre parent is cosmetic, two overlapping cards is a broken canvas. Measured live: 21 of 34 couples within 10px (was 6), 26 within 200px, 0 overlaps. Two refinements in 0.6.0: a CHILDLESS household keeps ELK's offset from its nearest sibling household that has children, rather than one row-wide average shift (the average let a bachelor uncle trade places with his married brother, who travelled further than the average); and an UNATTACHED household -- no recorded parents and no children -- goes to the end of its row, because measured in the browser an unlinked childless couple won the slot between two sets of grandparents by two pixels (x=214 against 216) and pushed the wife's parents out past it.
- **Siblings stand eldest first because ELK is handed them that way, not because a pass re-sorts them.** `pedigreeOrder()` in [lib/tree/layout.ts](lib/tree/layout.ts) walks the family depth-first from its oldest households, each with its partners beside it and its children sorted by birth year (people with a year first, by year; the rest in entry order), and that walk is the ELK model order, edges sorted the same way. ELK keeps model order wherever it costs no crossing, so the order IS the left-to-right reading of a generation. A single global sort by year would interleave cousins and hand ELK an order every couple's own children contradict. Measured: eldest-first sibling pairs 28/48 -> 47/48 on the earlier 117-person sample tree, 16/29 -> 29/29 mine-only, 0/12 -> 12/12 on a family entered youngest first; parent offset mean 192 -> 167px; 0 overlaps and 0 bar collisions at every level. The one remaining pair on that sample involved a person with two parent unions. The generation rails also print each row's recorded birth-year span (`GenerationBand.years`), so the levels read as a timeline.
- **Gap tiers are ratios of `metrics.gap`, so a level's gap has to make the TIERS legible, not merely stop labels touching.** Partner / household / family boundary run 22 / 56 / 141px at the card level, each step roughly 2.5x the last, so the eye groups on the largest gap before following a line; two tiers put a marriage and a family boundary both at 56 and fourteen people in one row read as one undifferentiated strip. The boundary is where the parent union changes, which is data rather than a heuristic on positions. The dot level's gap went 18 -> 30 for exactly this reason: at 18 the tiers computed to 20 / 35 / 79 against a 56px card, a partner gap a third of a card wide, so the level whose entire purpose is an overview showed no grouping at all.
- **An edge that meets a union must terminate on the dot's CENTRE, which React Flow will not do for you.** It anchors edges to HANDLES and offsets handles outward from the node box, so on a 12x12 junction a partner edge stopped 9px above the dot and the child edge began 9px below: measured 173 and 191 against a centre of 182, an 18px dead zone with the dot floating in it, which is the visibly broken line. The edge cannot fix it from inside, because by then `sourceY`/`targetY` ARE the handle positions -- so `TreeCanvas` passes each junction's centre down as `sourceHub`/`targetHub` and every generator snaps to it. Verified live: 150 of 150 junction endpoints on their dot centre, 0 off.
- **Testing `data` for truthiness is not the same as testing the field a branch needs.** Once every edge carried `data` for the junction anchor, partner edges (which have no sibling bar) fell into the elbow branch and read `bar.barY` as `undefined` -- emitting a path of literal `NaN`s whose `getTotalLength()` is 0, so the couple's line vanished entirely. The guard is `typeof bar.barY === "number"`.
- **A couple is joined by a horizontal MARRIAGE LINE at mid-card height, drawn by `partnerPath()` in [lib/tree/paths.ts](lib/tree/paths.ts), and the junction sits ON it (2026-08-07).** ELK places a union junction as an ordinary node in the generation gap to minimise crossings -- the right objective for a layered graph and the wrong position for a marriage, since the convention every hand-drawn pedigree uses is a line joining the couple with the children descending from its midpoint. `placeUnionJunctions()` in [lib/tree/layout.ts](lib/tree/layout.ts) (replacing `centreUnionDots()`) moves a couple's junction onto the line -- y at the partners' mid-card height, x from the couple's midpoint as the STARTING guess only. Since PR #32 the bead's x is clamped by `nearestClearPoint()` into the nearest clear gutter between the partners' facing edges, because a midpoint is only clear space when the couple is adjacent: with somebody laid out between them (a remarriage chain, a fused graph) it is the middle of that person's card, and 5 of 34 beads sat on people's faces. The partners' own cards count as blockers, so a bead never paints on a person card; a couple with NO clear gutter keeps ELK's y in the generation gap instead, the pre-marriage-line rendering. A single-parent union is centred under its one parent, where the honest picture is the classic straight drop. The junction bead wears the line's own colour and draws status in the genogram's conventions: a solid point for an intact partnership, a hollow bead for widowed, a double slash through the line for divorced or separated. Measured live: 68/68 partner edges flat, 34/34 beads on the line, 82/82 child drops leaving from the bead's centre, 0 NaN. A post-layout nudge for the same reason `siblingBars` is: the skeleton must not shift for cosmetics.
- **The dot level carries a FIRST name, and the width is measured rather than chosen.** A bare mark showed the shape of the family and nothing else, so finding anybody meant going back to cards and losing the overview. Across all 117 people of the earlier sample at 9px mono a first name needs 45px at p90 and 51px at the widest, where a full name needs 84px -- which would make this level nearly as wide as the compact row it exists to be smaller than. Hence a 56x34 slot, and `LEGIBLE_ZOOM.dot` raised 0.18 -> 0.42: at 0.18 a 9px label renders 1.6px tall, which is present in the DOM and unreadable, and that reads as a rendering fault rather than as an overview.
- **A connector's corners live in [lib/tree/paths.ts](lib/tree/paths.ts), because a hand-built path forgets to round them.** The sibling-bar route was assembled from `L` commands only, so 82 of 150 edges turned through hard 90 degree corners while `CORNER_RADIUS` sat unused in the fallback branch -- and one shared bar ran 6013px against a 200px median, a line crossing the whole canvas. Now every turn is a clamped quadratic (clamped to half of each leg, or a child almost directly below its parent bulges sideways past where it started), and the bracket's ends turn DOWN so it closes as one mark rather than reading as a rule. Verified live: hard-corner paths 82 -> 3, and the three remaining are straight vertical drops with no corner to round. Every family with two or more children draws the SAME bracket, however wide: 0.6.0 removed `MAX_BAR_SPAN` and the per-child curves wide families used to fall back to, because two shapes on one canvas read as two kinds of fact ("straight somewhere, curved elsewhere"), and curves all leaving one junction merged a wide family into a bundle where the bracket says exactly which children belong to whom. The old 6013px bar was mostly drift; with couples centred on their children a wide bar means a wide family. Verified live: 0 cubic `C` commands across 150 family paths in both frontends.
- **The orbit's angles were the defect, not its curves, and three tuning passes on the bow proved it.** Placing each ring by index alone gave a median edge span of 122 degrees with 26 of 34 edges crossing more than 90 -- a parent on ring 1 and its child on ring 2 got independent positions and routinely landed on opposite sides of the circle, so every edge had to cross the middle. Bowing them harder just turned chords into loops. Wedge allocation (each node placed inside its predecessor's angular sector) took the median to 1 degree and 0 over 90. **When a graph looks tangled, measure the angular separation before touching the path generator.**
- **In the orbit, wedges decide ORDER and even spacing decides GAPS, and conflating them makes the radius unbounded.** Sizing a ring on its tightest allocated slot solves `slot * r >= width + gap`, which grows without limit as a slot narrows: one crowded family took the outermost ring to 3641px, then to 9291px with a 17024px edge once unions were collapsed away. Capping the radius instead broke the no-overlap guarantee, because the two cannot both hold. So `ringRadius(count)` sets the radius and nodes are placed at even intervals in wedge order -- families stay contiguous (short edges) while every card gets an equal slot (no overlap). Final: max radius 1680px, longest edge 2817px, and 19/16/21/19 nodes across the four quadrants.
- **The orbit places PEOPLE only; union dots are traversed through, never ringed.** CLAUDE.md already says a union dot is "the MARRIAGE, not a route to the children", and this view takes that literally: counting a junction as a hop put a spouse alone on ring 1 while parents and children shared ring 2, and it spent a full 168px ring step on a 12x12 mark every other generation. `collapseUnionEdges()` rewrites partner and child edges person-to-person (2 + N becomes 1 + 2N, affordable because the orbit shows at most four rings), and `hopsFrom` takes an `isUnion` predicate so a ring means "people I am N relationships from".
- **3D is applied to the CARD, never to the pane, and per-card is also the version that works.** A canvas-wide `perspective` + `rotateX` was the obvious implementation and is wrong: React Flow owns each node wrapper's 2D `translate`, so tilting an ancestor leaves the layout and the picture disagreeing about where a card is, and the SVG brackets (drawn in flat coordinates) no longer meet the cards they join. Per-card was verified empirically rather than reasoned about -- a card under `rotateX(45deg)` measured 132px -> 94px, three hit-probes down its face all landed inside it, and its click still fired, because the browser's own hit-testing follows a CSS transform. Confirmed on the real canvas at 151 tilted nodes: 13 of 14 visible tilted cards clickable, the exception sitting under a toolbar panel.
- **A relation edge must never influence node placement.** `FlowEdge.layout` is the discriminator and [lib/tree/layout.ts](lib/tree/layout.ts) filters on it before handing edges to ELK. Feed a "friend" edge to the layered algorithm and that friend gets shoved into a lower generation, which silently misrepresents the family. A layout test asserted the family skeleton's positions are identical with and without relation edges present; the suite is gone, but the invariant stands -- if positions shift when the overlay is fed in, it leaked into the skeleton.
- **Withholding social edges from ELK creates a second problem, which `anchorFamilylessNodes()` in layout.ts solves.** A friend with no parents and no partner is an isolated node to ELK, so it lands in the FIRST layer and renders as somebody older than the grandparents. The post-layout pass moves those people onto the row of a contact they know. It deliberately runs after layout instead of as an ELK constraint, so the skeleton cannot shift to accommodate a friend.
- **Generation bands come from person nodes only.** A union dot sits between two rows, so including one invents a half-generation. The entrance stagger therefore snaps each node to its NEAREST band rather than an exact y match, or every union dot gets delay 0 and pops in ahead of the couple it joins.
- **The sibling bar is computed from POSITIONS, after ELK, never as an edge.** ELK routes parent-child edges individually, so twelve children produce twelve separate diagonals; the bar is the thing that makes them read as one family. Each lane takes the most families that can share it (earliest-finishing first), lane 0 first, which keeps a generation to a single clean line wherever it can -- because two families sharing a y AND an x range is the one way the bar can lie about whose child is whose. Lane 0 runs through the MIDDLE of the gap (equal drops above and below, the 0.8.0 fix for a bracket sitting 9px above the children); the rest alternate either side. Lanes are unbounded since 0.8.0: the Wikidata sample's intermarried dynasties put six families' children over one x, and the old cap of three wrapped them onto shared lines. Since 0.8.1 a lane holds a family's WHOLE run -- the bracket plus the stems from its junction, which can lie beyond the children's span; spacing it by the children alone let two families' stems share a line -- and a lone child who is not straight below the junction turns through a lane too, instead of React Flow's smoothstep, whose run landed wherever the midpoint fell (one ran 18px under its parents' row for 5000px). Spacing is FIXED, `rowGap / 8` (9px on cards), and every line stays `rowGap / 3` clear of both rows (24px on cards); a crowded gap is made taller rather than squeezed (`gapGrowth`, applied by `openCrowdedGaps` in layout.ts, which moves only the rows below it). Squeezing put six lanes 7.2px apart with the outer ones 13px from the cards, which read as lines touching the boxes. Measured on 0.8.0 in production: 13 pairs of lines from different families on one y; after, none at any level, and the closest line to a card is 24px.
- **A bar can never sit above its own union dot.** With a tight generation gap the lane offset exceeds the room available, and the drop line then points UPWARDS, reading as the children being the ancestors. Since 0.8.1 a family is simply not offered a lane above its junction; the old clamp to just below the dot moved two families onto one y. A single parent's junction is placed ON its children's bar afterwards (`seatSingleParents`), so the parent's line drops to a bead on the bracket; it used to sit mid-gap, exactly where lane 0 runs.

## Repo-specific rules

- **Card width AND height both come from `NODE_METRICS`, because ELK reserves both.** `PersonNode` set only the width, so the card sized itself to content -- raising the reserved height to fit a two-line kinship term then bought nothing on screen and left the layout and the card disagreeing by 16px (reserved 92, rendered 78, measured live). The inner card needs `size-full` for the same reason.
- **The kinship line clamps to TWO lines; the name never wraps.** Measured on the earlier 117-person sample tree: 31 of 117 kinship terms (26%) were truncated at one line, because that term is the widest thing on the card -- "great-great-uncle by marriage" needs 164px in a 142px column, where the p90 NAME needs 109px. Truncating kinship is worse than truncating anything else, since the terms share long prefixes and "great-great-gran..." could be a grandmother or a grandfather. `kinship.ts` is already maximally compressed (greats collapse to `4x great-` past three), so the fix belongs in the card.
- **Do NOT put a glyph on the name line.** Tried, measured, reverted: the sex glyph plus its gap takes the name column from 142px to 105px, which pushed 9 of the earlier sample's 117 names from fitting into truncating. The name is the one thing on a card that must never be clipped. Glyphs that are on EVERY card go on the metadata row; the name line carries only conditional marks (provenance tick, conflict shield).
